We wish to submit our original research article entitled "Characterising measurements of vessel diameter in optical coherence tomography angiography and correcting artefact-induced underestimation" for consideration by Biomedical Optics Express.

Optical coherence tomography angiography metrics are widely used to quantify retinal vascular structure and function, yet the accuracy of retinal vessel diameter measurements can be strongly affected by how angiographic images are processed. In this work, we identify a systematic vessel diameter underestimation and demonstrate that its effect can be large enough to alter the interpretation of vascular responses.

We show that commonly used global threshold or binary-mask-based measurements and maximum intensity projections underestimate vessel diameter because the optical signal at the vessel edges is reduced by the sheer induced red blood cell alignment. We further demonstrate that model-based diameter calculation provides reliable diameter estimates, and that the remaining systematic bias can be corrected through calibration.

Importantly, we found that this is not simply a constant measurement offset. In a retinal flicker-stimulation experiment, conventional binary-mask-based measurements indicated apparent vessel constriction, whereas model-based measurements correctly captured the expected vasodilation. This demonstrates that the artefact can affect not only quantitative measurements but also the physiological conclusions drawn from OCTA experiments.

We believe this work is well suited to Biomedical Optics Express because it addresses a systematic underestimation issue and provides practical approaches for more reliable vessel diameter measurements. The findings are relevant to researchers using vessel calibre, be it in the form of vessel diameter or density, as an outcome measure in studies of retinal vascular function and diseases.

We wish to submit our original research article entitled "Characterising measurements of vessel diameter in optical coherence tomography angiography and correcting artefact-induced underestimation" for consideration by Biomedical Optics Express.

Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) is widely used to quantify the retinal vasculature, and many of its metrics depend on accurate measurement of vessel diameter. In this work, we show that measured diameter depends systematically on how the angiogram is calculated. Shear induced alignment of red blood cells with the flow reduces backscattering at the vessel edges, causing these edges to fall below the threshold used to define the vessel boundary and resulting in systematic diameter underestimation.

To quantify this, we established ground truth diameters in the in vivo mouse retina using Intralipid contrast enhancement, which restores the suppressed edge signal. We compared conventional binary-mask-based measurement with fitting a model to the vessel's intensity profile. Model-based fitting was the more reliable of the two. Without contrast, it underestimated diameter, but the bias was predictable enough to be corrected by calibration, whereas the binary-mask-based measurement was not. We also found that mean-intensity projection through the vessel depth provides more accurate diameter estimates than conventional maximum-intensity projection.

Importantly, in a flicker-stimulation experiment, binary-mask-based measurements tracked the declining angiogram intensity and indicated vasoconstriction, whereas model-based fitting detected the expected vasodilation. Thus, this artefact can alter not only quantitative vessel diameter measurements but also the inferred physiological conclusions, with implications for studies comparing vessels across or within imaging sessions. 

We believe this work is well suited to Biomedical Optics Express because it characterises a systematic imaging artefact and provides practical measures for improving vessel-diameter quantification in OCTA. The findings are relevant to researchers using vessel calibre or vessel density as outcome measures in studies of retinal vascular function and disease.
